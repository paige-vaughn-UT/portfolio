Consumer growth · Scalable market strategy · Integrated storytelling

Making one experience relevant in twelve different markets.

A location-based business needs local demand. A national brand needs a national story. The problem was building one system that produced both, all year.

01 The problem

A location business with a national brand has two audiences and one budget.

Twelve tunnels, each needing bookings from people within driving distance. One brand, needing enough national presence that the concept is familiar before anyone sees a local listing. Run those as separate programs and you get expensive duplication. Run only the national program and no tunnel sells a ticket.

The second constraint was novelty. Indoor skydiving is inherently visual and inherently one-note: it is fun, and then the story is over. A program that relies on the experience being novel produces one wave of coverage and then silence.

Both problems have the same answer. Stop pitching the tunnel. Build a small number of story platforms - durable reasons the experience is relevant to a defined audience - and run each one nationally and locally, on a schedule.

The strategic idea

Stop selling the experience. Build the reasons it matters, then repeat them on a calendar.

Four platforms, each tied to an audience with a recurring need and a predictable news moment - which is what turns a one-off activation into a year-round engine that any market can run.

Why platforms instead of campaigns

A campaign is spent once. A platform can be re-pitched every year with a new local proof point, a new spokesperson, or a new market - and it accumulates relationships with the reporters who cover that beat.

What that made possible operationally

Any of the twelve markets could run any platform with a local face and a local detail, using the same underlying angle, briefing material, and proof points.

Local execution stopped requiring central strategy every time.

03 The engine

Coverage every month of the program, not one spike.

The test of a platform system is whether it produces a drumbeat. Across the 2017 program, placements landed in every month from April to December - with local clusters where a market activation ran and national peaks aligned to the back-to-school and gifting moments.

What the evidence supports

Thirty-five placements across nine months, twelve markets, and eleven initiatives - with the top coverage drivers being exactly the four platforms the strategy was built on: STEM and back-to-school, holiday, All Abilities, and the sport of indoor skydiving.

The program also reported $3.67M+ in advertising value equivalency, a metric the industry has since largely abandoned, and which I would not present as a result today.

What I would measure instead now

  • Market-level demand lift - bookings or search volume in a market in the four weeks after a local placement, against unsupported markets as a control
  • Platform yield - placements and quality per platform, to decide which of the four to fund next year and which to retire
  • Message penetration - what share of coverage carried the intended relevance angle rather than describing the venue

All three are answerable with data a client already has, and all three turn an earned-media program into a growth program.
