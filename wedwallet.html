Creating the market for event financial software.

The founders understood finance and software. I understood the market. My job was to turn a technical payments product into a category the wedding industry could recognize, trust, and repeat back.

01 The problem

A modern wedding experience still ran on outdated payment operations.

Luxury weddings had been redesigned in nearly every dimension - planning, design, guest experience, documentation. The money had not. Couples, planners, and vendors were still moving six- and seven-figure sums through wire transfers, spreadsheets, bank portals, consumer payment apps, and scattered email confirmations.

Founder Andrea came from payments and fintech and saw the gap clearly as an infrastructure problem. What the company did not yet have was a way to describe the problem in language the wedding industry already used - or a view of which audience had to move first.

That is the gap I was hired into.

The insight that set the category

Weddings this expensive stop behaving like celebrations and start behaving like financial projects.

Once the event is framed as a project with contracts, milestones, approvals, and an audit trail, the product stops competing with payment apps and starts competing with the spreadsheet - a much easier argument to win, and a much better one for pricing.

02 My contribution

I was the bridge between the product they built and the market they needed.

The mandate

Define the market narrative. Clarify the customer value. Build the launch system. Bring industry judgment into product and brand decisions.

  • Industry expertise - planner relationships, vendor payment realities, and the etiquette around money in high-value celebrations
  • Market strategy - audience definition, category positioning, message architecture, launch sequencing, partnership strategy
  • Execution leadership - press materials, executive positioning, social and video strategy, publishing, cross-channel launch
  • Product input - translating market expectations back into what the product and site needed to say and show
03 The questions

Four decisions had to be made before anything could be written.

A complex fintech product had to become understandable and desirable inside a relationship-driven luxury market. Each question below had a wrong answer that would have been expensive to discover after launch.

  1. 01

    What category should WedWallet own?

    Payment app, planning tool, bank-like service, or a new operating layer for wedding finance? The answer determined the competitive set, the pricing logic, and the objection we would spend the launch answering.

  2. 02

    Who needs to believe first?

    Couples control the money, planners influence adoption, vendors determine whether the workflow is practical. Sequencing these wrong stalls adoption at the first handoff.

  3. 03

    How does a new brand earn financial trust?

    The story had to explain custody, authorization, privacy, and records without losing the emotional relevance of the occasion, and without the generic security language that reads as evasive in fintech.

  4. 04

    How should the market launch?

    Press, founder visibility, social content, video, and partnerships each needed a distinct job inside one narrative, rather than four versions of the same announcement.

04 Category

Reframing the competitive set.

What the evidence said

The market was not shopping for a better payment app. It was already coping - with spreadsheets, bank portals, consumer payment apps, and email threads used as the record of what had been agreed and paid.

The pain was not the transaction. It was reconstructing the state of the project: what is due, what was approved, what has been paid, and where the proof lives.

What I did about it

Positioned WedWallet as modern payments infrastructure for ultra-luxury events - purpose-built for a high-stakes event workflow, not a general-purpose wallet.

  • Competitive set moved from payment apps to the spreadsheet-and-email status quo
  • Value argument moved from convenience to control and record-keeping
  • Language moved from fintech feature names to the industry's own vocabulary
Audience strategy

One product. Three adoption problems.

The single most consequential call in the engagement. WedWallet does not have one customer - it has three parties with different relationships to the money, and a message that satisfies one can actively alarm another.

Couples & families

Control the funds

They need confidence that every contribution and payment is visible, authorized, and documented - including the family money that arrives with expectations attached.

Adoption risk: handing money to an unfamiliar brand.

Planners

Coordinate the workflow

They need operational visibility without holding client funds, and without chasing screenshots and confirmations to prove a vendor was paid.

Adoption risk: looking like a liability, not a tool.

Vendors

Receive payment

They need a secure, low-friction process that fits existing business operations and does not slow down the deposit that lets them book the date.

Adoption risk: one more system they did not ask for.

Why this mattered

Planners are the distribution channel. They are consulted before the couple chooses tools and they carry the workflow across dozens of events a year, so a message that wins the planner compounds while a message that wins one couple does not.

What I did about it

Every piece of messaging had to answer three questions explicitly: who controls the money, who sees the workflow, and who receives payment.

Launch sequencing led with planner credibility - a vendor verification badge and a founding planners program - rather than consumer acquisition.

05 Positioning

Four pillars, one promise.

The narrative paired financial control with the emotional relief of removing payment friction from a major life event. Each pillar exists to answer a specific objection.

The positioning system
PillarThe claimThe objection it answers
CategoryPayment infrastructure built for high-value events, not another general payment app."We already have a way to pay people."
FunctionOne operating record. Contracts, due dates, approvals, transfers, and receipts stay connected."Our spreadsheet works fine."
TrustControl before money moves. Customers authorize each payment while funds stay separate through sponsor bank infrastructure."Who is actually holding my money?"
ExperienceLess money talk. Couples and planners spend less energy reconstructing payment status across tools."This is one more thing to manage."
06 Product translation

The dashboard is where the strategy became legible.

The product story focused on operational visibility rather than abstract fintech features. What the market needed to see was a shared ledger, vendor-level context, and a clear control model - so that is what the interface story led with.

A shared ledger

Couples and planners work from the same payment record, which removes the screenshot economy between them.

Vendor-level context

Contracts, invoices, balances, approvals, and receipts stay attached to the vendor they belong to.

A clear control model

The client authorizes payment while the planner retains visibility - the distinction that makes planners safe to adopt it.

07 Trust

Financial credibility could not rely on generic claims.

So the messaging explained the mechanics instead of asserting safety.

Fund separationCustomer funds remain separate through sponsor bank infrastructure.
Explicit approvalPayments move only after the customer authorizes them.
Complete recordsEach payment leaves a timestamped, downloadable record.

09 Partnership

I brokered WedWallet's first philanthropic partnership.

VOW for Girls gave a new brand a mission-aligned way to connect weddings with a couple's first act of giving as a new family - and connected WedWallet to a nonprofit already respected inside the wedding industry.

What I owned

Identified the opportunity, brokered the relationship, shaped the shared narrative, and moved the partnership toward launch content.

10 Impact

A technical product entered the market with language the industry could use.

What happened

WedWallet announced its soft launch on November 12, 2025, using the trust and transparency narrative developed during the engagement.

The live site still carries the audience logic, operating-system framing, security explanation, and planner-led workflow I helped shape - the positioning outlasted the contract.

What I am not claiming

I do not have post-launch revenue, sign-up, activation, or retention data, so no performance outcome is asserted here.

The verifiable results are the launch itself, the market-facing system that produced it, and the positioning still in use.
